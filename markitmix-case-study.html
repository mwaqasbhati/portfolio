01 — The starting point

It started with a product idea, not a codebase.

There was no backlog to work through and no design system to implement. There was a business that needed to exist: sellers who wanted somewhere to list and sell, buyers who wanted to browse, bid and buy, and an operator who needed to keep all of it running.

The real challenge was never "build some screens". It was to make a marketplace business work end to end — multiple vendors, their catalogues, customers, carts that mix sellers, auctions with real money attached, payments that have to reach the right party, and the day-to-day operational workflows behind all of it. Every screen had to sit on top of rules that made commercial sense first.

04 — The hard part

One checkout. Multiple vendors.

This is where a marketplace stops behaving like a shop. A customer fills a single cart without caring who sells what — but the moment they pay, that one transaction has to be understood as several separate pieces of business, each belonging to a different vendor.

The business problem

If a buyer adds three items from three different sellers, the platform needs to know exactly which product belongs to which vendor, create the right order records for each of them, take one payment from the customer, and then make sure the money is distributed to the correct parties — with the platform's own position accounted for.

Get it wrong and you do not get a bug report. You get an angry seller who was not paid, or a buyer charged for something nobody is fulfilling.

Decision and implementation

I designed and implemented the multi-vendor checkout and split-payment flow: cart items resolved back to their vendors, orders derived per vendor from a single purchase, and payment distribution handled as part of the order lifecycle instead of as a manual back-office chore. The flow was designed to operate with 50+ vendors on the platform.

Stripe marketplace payment flow

This was not a basic checkout button integration. The system had to align seller account connection, vendor-specific order state, payment attribution, and post-payment order lifecycle handling so finance and fulfilment stayed in sync.

Edge conditions such as partial fulfilment changes or order adjustments were treated as payment-state problems as much as order-state problems.

05 — Smart shipping

One seller. Multiple purchases. One smarter shipment.

A buyer may purchase multiple products from the same seller. Treating each item as an independent shipment increases cost and operational overhead for both sides.

Without bundlingEach item creates a separate shipment flow
Item A → shipmentSeller-level handling repeated
Item B → shipmentExtra operational steps
Item C → shipmentHigher shipping cost exposure
With MarkitMix bundlingItems evaluated within seller context
Item A + Item B + Item CSame seller / eligible items
Combined shipment flowLower friction for buyer and seller

This mattered commercially as well as technically: better checkout economics, cleaner operations, and fewer avoidable fulfilment decisions for sellers.

06 — Auctions

From marketplace listings to live auctions

Auctions were not a side feature. They gave vendors a second way to sell and gave the marketplace a reason for customers to come back — but they had to land in exactly the same order and payment machinery as a normal purchase.

The listing model supports buy-it-now items, multiple-item listings, smart bundle savings on shipment, pickup flows, scheduled drops, time-based auctions and flash sales in one consistent experience.

Under the hood: keeping bids consistent

The difficult part was not showing a countdown. The difficult part was state consistency when bids arrive close together.

The system had to validate each incoming bid against the latest auction state, decide whether it is accepted, update the live state, and persist that state so the eventual winner and order creation are unambiguous.

This is where engineering discipline matters: stale state and race conditions can create false winners, broken trust and payment disputes.

08 — AI where it adds value

AI as part of the product — not just a chatbot

AI was introduced where it removed real workflow friction, using OpenAI and Claude. The bar was practical: if it did not save real manual effort in a product flow, it stayed out.

Listing assistance in the seller workflow

Problem

Listings were often thin or inconsistent when sellers were entering them quickly.

Input

Seller-provided item details from the listing form.

Model output

Structured copy suggestions and clearer product wording.

Control

The seller remains in control and decides what is published.

Catalog consistency across many vendors

Problem

Catalogue quality drifts when many sellers describe similar items differently.

Why AI

Language models can help normalize and structure free-form product information.

In product

Applied in catalogue flows to improve customer-side consistency without forcing sellers into rigid manual editing.

The principle stayed the same throughout: AI is useful when it improves a real business workflow. Anything that could not be tied back to a workflow did not make it into the product.
